Why Quality Assurance Decides Project Success

26 June 2026 · RAIT Solution

Software defects found after go-live cost up to ten times more than those caught during development. Structured quality assurance is not optional — it is an investment with measurable ROI.

The problem: QA is planned too late — or not at all

In many IT projects, quality assurance is treated as an afterthought. Only once development is finished does the team "quickly run a few tests" — often under time pressure, with unclear requirements and no structured test plan. The result: bugs are found too late, go-live is postponed and the budget runs out.

The root of the problem is that QA is often seen as a cost factor rather than an investment in quality. Those who invest in quality assurance early save many times more later on.

What professional quality assurance really means

Good QA does not begin with the first test case — it begins with the requirements. A structured QA process includes:

  • Test planning and test strategy — What is tested, to what depth and with which tools?
  • Requirements-based test cases — Every requirement has measurable acceptance criteria
  • Defect management — Bugs are classified, prioritised and tracked
  • User acceptance testing (UAT) — The business signs off in a structured way, not "on demand"
  • Test documentation — Indispensable for audits, compliance and future maintenance

The cost of poor quality assurance

The consequences of inadequate QA are well documented: production outages, data loss, reputational damage and costly rework. In regulated sectors such as insurance or public administration, compliance risks come on top.

A realistic example: a defect in the requirements phase costs around 1. The same defect in development costs around 10. After go-live it costs 100 or more — plus reputational damage and business interruption.

ISTQB as the quality standard

The International Software Testing Qualifications Board (ISTQB) has developed internationally recognised standards for software testing. ISTQB-certified testers and test managers share a common understanding of processes, methods and terminology — which makes collaboration on the project easier and ensures traceable results.
